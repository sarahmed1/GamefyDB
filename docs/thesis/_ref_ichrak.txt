presented to users in a format that is consistent, credible, and ready for visualization
or decision support systems.

2.5.3 Data Warehouse Design Approaches
Data warehouse design is a crucial step of setting up a robust BI system. Data warehouse
is a centralized database storing historical, consolidated, subject-oriented data to support
reporting and analytical operations. Two major methodologies have been developed over
time for designing a data warehouse: the Inmon method and the Kimball method. Both
offer varying approaches to structuring the data and mapping it with business needs.

2.5.3.1 Inmon Approach
The Inmon approach, created by Bill Inmon, is the top-down approach. It begins with
the development of a centralized, normalized enterprise data warehouse (EDW) that data
marts are then developed from as and when needed. Data integration and consistency are
given prime importance with all data standardized to enterprise-wide standards before it
is used for reporting [5].
Figure 2.4: Inmon Approach architecture [6].
2.5.3.2 Kimball Approach
The Kimball approach, supported by Ralph Kimball, is also called the bottom-up ap-
proach to data warehouse design. It is about building data marts around specific business
processes (e.g., transportation, sales, finance) and aggregating them later at a central
data warehouse. The method relies heavily on the application of the dimensional model-
ing technique, where data is organized in fact and dimension tables, usually in a star or
snowflake schema [7].

2.5.3.3 Adopted Approach
For this project, the Kimball methodology was used. The reasons for the usage were the
need for fast access to insight, business-driven KPIs as high-priority, and modularity of
the transport processes being analyzed. The bottom-up approach facilitated the rapid
development of subject-specific data marts that aligned with the organization’s short-
term analytical needs. The simplicity of the dimensional model and performance gains
also qualified it as the optimal solution for building interactive dashboards and deliver-
ing an end-user-friendly reporting experience to stakeholders such as HR and operations
managers.

3.4.3 Data Transformation Phase (Transform)
Before any analysis occurs, the data must first be transformed. This step is important
to prepare the data for analysis by cleaning, standardizing, and structuring it to improve
quality and consistency.
• Identifying data quality issues: During our initial review of the data source,
we noted several quality issues. First, we found inconsistencies in some references,
pointing to deleted records, leading to a loss of referential integrity and a risk of an-
alytical errors. Second, some information appeared multiple times in many entries,
creating unnecessary redundancies, thus increasing the risk of inconsistencies during
updates. Third, some columns consisted exclusively of non-descriptive identifiers,
making interpretation difficult without first applying transformations or contextual
mappings. For non-descriptive identifiers, this can complicate the understanding of
the data, but also prevent direct analysis. Also, a structural issue was identified with
the depiction of hierarchical relationships. In particular, equating entities possessing

4.5. DEFINITION OF GENERATIVE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
4.5 Definition of generative Artificial Intelligence
Generative AI is a specific sub-discipline of the broad artificial intelligence field that
is designed to generate new and original content through learning from a vast pool of
availableinformation. IncontrasttootherAI,whichhasprimarilybeenemployedfortasks
such as classification, prediction, or decision-making based merely on already available
information, generative AI goes the extra step by generating content that has not been
available before. Legacy systems are only able to identify patterns or provide suggestions,
whereas generative AI systems are capable of creating new material entirely in any number
of varying formssuch as, but notlimited to, pictures, writing, sound, video, code, anddata
visualizations. The technology is evolving continuously and will probably generate even
more types of outputs that have not been conceived or even thought of yet. Generative
AI is generally built on top of cutting-edge deep learning frameworks, and the most
widely used one currently is the transformer model due to its superior performance and
adaptability. These models are trained with large-scale, well-curated datasets that allow
them to abstract the underlying patterns, structures, and relationships in the input data.
As a result, generative AI can produce output not just coherent and contextually accurate,
but also stylistically precise to human-authored content, whether in language or any other
well-structured medium.

4.6.3 Working Mechanism of the Mistral-Saba-24b Model
The Mistral-Saba-24b is built upon a highly advanced transformer architecture that has
been specifically developed to maximize large-scale natural language processing. When
it receives a query from a user via the interface, the model first breaks down the text
into semantic units called tokens, allowing for an extensive examination of the input.
With its training on massive datasets, Mistral then goes ahead to understand the general
meaning of the query, identifying the user’s purpose and key factors necessary for cor-
rect comprehension. The system applies preprocessing reasoning to categorize the query,
routing it to appropriate SQL queries or relevant processing procedures. Based on this,
the model constructs a structured text response that may comprise sophisticated data
analysis, personalized suggestions, or instructions for designing interactive visualizations.
In the interest of ensuring a fast and effective user experience, a caching mechanism holds
the result of repetitive queries so that unnecessary API calls are avoided and response
rates are improved. Mistral also capitalizes on the context enabled by data stored in a star
schema so that it can return consistent and relevant responses, for example, suggesting
actions based on trends observed, thereby enhancing the usability and relevance of the
insights being served.

6.4 Conception
This section focuses on our system design phase, during which we define the structural and

system components communicate and how data is structured and processed. To this end,

6.4.1 Sequence Diagrams
The sequence diagram shows process interactions in order of time. The diagram depicts
the objects and processes utilized and messages passed in sequence that are required to
realize the functionality.
6.4.1.1 Scan Badge sequence diagram
The sequence diagram in figure 6.5 illustrates the badge scanning process in the system
to activate an employee to use the transport service by scanning his/her badge. It starts
when the employee scans his/her badge and the system authenticates it. It initially
checks whether the badge has already been scanned before. If it did, the system denies by
displaying a message the badge has been scanned previously, denies access, sends an alert,
and updates the unauthorized scan list. In case it is a new scan, the system then verifies
whether the employee is authorized to use the transportation. If the employee is not
authorized, access is denied and the unauthorized list is updated. But in the event that
the authorized employee, the system gives access, lists on the approved list, and records
the scan. This system keeps only approved staff members able to use the transport service,
prevents the repetition of scans, and maintains a record of both approved and unapproved
accesses.
